Have you ever closed a trade and felt that you had to enter again?

Maybe you wanted to recover a loss. Maybe you missed a move. Maybe the chart was quiet and you felt that you should be doing something.

That urge is common. The important part is what you do next.

[Overtrading](/glossary/overtrading) happens when your trading starts to move away from the plan you wrote before the pressure appeared. You may take more trades, stay longer than planned, accept a weaker setup, or change your size because of the last result.

There is no single number of trades that means you are overtrading. You need to compare each decision with your own plan.

A [prop firm challenge](/glossary/prop-firm-challenge) is an evaluation where you trade under the firm's account rules. Those rules can add pressure, but they do not decide whether you overtraded. Your written process and your recorded actions provide that evidence.

## First, do not judge yourself by the trade count

You may take five trades and follow your plan on all five. You may also break your plan on the second trade.

The number alone does not tell you enough. Check what changed.

| Check this | Still following your plan | The plan may be slipping |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Every entry meets the same written conditions | You accept a weaker reason to enter |
| Session | You trade only in your planned time window | You stay for another session because you do not want to stop |
| Confirmation | You wait for the same final check each time | You enter before that check is complete |
| Size | You use the same recorded sizing method | You change size because of the last win or loss |
| Reason | You can explain the entry before you know the result | Your reason is mainly “one more,” “make it back,” or “I missed it” |

This is why patience matters. Patience does not mean sitting still without a plan. It means waiting until the conditions you already chose are present.

The [trading discipline guide](/blogs/trading-discipline-prop-firm) explains how your rules, journal, and review can work together. Here, we will focus only on spotting overtrading.

## Look at this fictional XAUUSD session

Imagine that you trade XAUUSD and record time in IST.

Your plan says:

- trade only during the London session;
- wait for an Asian-session liquidity sweep, which means price briefly moves beyond an earlier high or low;
- enter only after a breakout candle closes, which means the candle finishes beyond your marked level.

![Step 1 handwritten XAUUSD plan showing the Asian high and waiting for the London session](/blogs/images/overtrading-step-1-plan.webp)

Your first trade follows all three checks. It ends at **−$38**.

The loss does not prove that the trade was bad. You followed the process you wrote.

![Step 2 handwritten XAUUSD example showing the London liquidity sweep, breakout close, and recorded loss of $38](/blogs/images/overtrading-step-2-trade.webp)

Later, during the New York session, you see price moving again. You feel the urge to recover the London loss. You enter before the breakout candle closes.

![Step 3 handwritten XAUUSD review comparing the earlier London trade with an unfinished New York candle and the urge to recover](/blogs/images/overtrading-step-3-urge.webp)

Now pause and look at the facts:

1. You moved outside your planned session.
2. You did not wait for the final check.
3. The last loss became part of your reason for entering.

That second trade needs review. Its profit or loss will not change those three facts.

## Five signs you can check in your journal

### 1. Your trade count changes after a win or loss

Ask yourself: did another planned setup appear, or did the last result make me want another trade?

Write down the trade number, the previous result, the setup name, and your reason for entry. During a later [trade review](/glossary/trade-review)—a calm check of trades you already took—you can compare what usually happens after wins, losses, and missed moves.

### 2. You start accepting weaker setups

At the start of the session, you may wait for every check. Later, you may call something a “breakout” even though the candle has not closed.

Use [setup compliance](/glossary/setup-compliance) fields to record whether the trade met the conditions you wrote. Do not grade the setup by profit or loss. A winning rule break is still a rule break. A losing trade can still follow the plan.

### 3. You stay longer than planned

You may plan to trade London, take a loss, and then keep watching into New York because stopping feels difficult.

Record the session and timezone for every trade. IST helps you compare your Asian, London, and New York entries. Use the session times in your own current plan because overseas daylight-saving changes can move them in IST.

### 4. The last trade becomes the reason for the next trade

Notes such as “make it back,” “one more,” and “missed the move” tell you how you felt. They do not describe a trading setup.

Keep those notes. They are useful evidence.

If you act mainly because of a loss or frustration, read the meaning of [revenge trading](/glossary/revenge-trading). If you act because you fear missing a move, read about [FOMO](/glossary/fomo), or fear of missing out. Either feeling can lead to overtrading, but they are not the same thing.

### 5. Your notes become less clear

“Looked good” is hard to review.

“Entered XAUUSD during New York after my planned London session. The breakout candle had not closed” tells you exactly what changed.

Use the same names for your instrument, session, setup, and final check. Clear notes make it harder for memory to rewrite the story later.

## What to do when you feel the urge to trade

You do not need a complicated system. Use this short check before the next entry.

### Step 1: Name what you feel

Write one honest line: “I want to recover the loss,” “I missed the move,” or “I am bored.”

This does not mean you are weak or that the next trade must fail. It simply records what is happening before you decide.

### Step 2: Check your written setup

Ask:

- Is this one of my named setups?
- Am I inside my planned session?
- Is my final confirmation complete?
- Am I using my normal sizing method?

If you cannot answer from your written plan, do not invent the rule while the chart is moving. Record the gap and review it later.

### Step 3: Keep the result separate

Record the result in USD, but do not use it as the score for your decision.

Also record:

- planned, partly planned, or unplanned;
- setup checks completed;
- session and IST time;
- what you felt before entry;
- whether the previous trade affected your choice;
- any change in your normal size or confirmation.

The [trading journal template](/blogs/trading-journal-template) gives you these fields, an example row, and a CSV download.

### Step 4: Review the pattern later

Do not try to solve the whole problem while you feel pressure to trade.

During your weekly review, look for repeated facts:

- Which trades were outside your planned session?
- Did you stop waiting for confirmation after a loss or missed move?
- Did your setup names become vague later in the day?
- Did you change your normal size?
- Which feelings appeared before unplanned trades?

Pick one pattern to watch during the next review period. Keep it small enough to check from your journal.

## Add simple controls to your own rulebook

Your strategy and prop firm account may be different from someone else's. Choose controls that fit your own written process.

Examples include:

- a clear session end time;
- a setup checklist you complete before entry;
- a pause after you record a rule break;
- a rule to write your reason before another trade after a strong emotion;
- fixed labels for planned, partly planned, and unplanned trades;
- a set time for your end-of-session or weekly review.

These are examples, not trading instructions or universal limits. Check the current rules for your own prop firm program.

## Common mistakes to avoid during review

**Calling every busy day overtrading.** A busy day can still follow your plan.

**Using profit as proof.** A winning trade can break your rules. A losing trade can follow them.

**Choosing a random daily trade limit.** A number only makes sense when it fits your strategy and account rules.

**Changing setup names after entry.** Use the same labels so you can compare similar trades.

**Recording only difficult days.** Keep the same basic notes on calm days too. You need both for a fair comparison.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many trades count as overtrading?

There is no universal number. Compare your trade count, session, setup, confirmation, size, and reason with your written plan.

### Is a second trade after a loss always overtrading?

No. The second trade may still follow your setup and session rules. Check whether the loss changed your reason, size, timing, or final confirmation.

### Can a profitable trade still be overtrading?

Yes. Profit tells you the result. Overtrading tells you that your process changed. Record them separately.

### Should an Indian forex trader record results in INR?

PropLogAI forex and prop-firm examples use USD. You can still use IST for session times and your local review routine.

### What is the difference between overtrading and revenge trading?

Revenge trading starts with a reaction to a loss or frustration. Overtrading is the change you can see in trade count, timing, size, or setup quality. Revenge trading can lead to overtrading, but not every overtrading pattern starts with a loss.

When you feel the urge to take another trade, you do not need to argue with yourself. Pause, check the plan you already wrote, and record what changed. That gives your future review something real to work with.
