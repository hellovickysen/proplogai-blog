You close an XAUUSD trade during the London session. You remember that it made **+$100**, but a day later you cannot clearly explain why you entered, whether you followed your plan, or what you should review.

That is the problem a useful [trading journal](/glossary/trading-journal) solves.

It does more than store entry, exit, and profit or loss. It keeps the plan, what you actually did, the result, and the lesson in one place.

> **Quick answer:** before the trade, record your setup, session, entry reason, planned risk, stop, and the condition that would cancel the idea. After the trade, record what happened, whether you followed the plan, how you felt, and one short lesson. At the end of the week, look for one repeated behaviour to work on.

## What should a prop firm trading journal help you understand?

Your journal should help you answer four questions:

1. **What was the plan?**
2. **What did I actually do?**
3. **What was the result?**
4. **What can I learn from the difference?**

Keep these questions separate. A profitable trade can contain a rule break. A losing trade can still follow the plan.

This matters in a prop-firm challenge or funded account because you may also need to review account rules, daily limits, session restrictions, or your own sizing method. The exact rule depends on your firm and program. Your journal records what you checked; it does not replace the firm's current dashboard or agreement.

If you need the rule-specific version, use the separate [funded-account journal workflow](/blogs/forex-journal-funded-accounts). This guide stays focused on the basic plan, trade, and review loop.

## What should you record before an XAUUSD trade?

Before you enter, write enough detail to judge the decision later without using the result as your answer.

Start with:

- **Instrument:** XAUUSD.
- **Session:** Asian, London, or New York.
- **Setup name:** for example, Asian low liquidity sweep.
- **Direction:** buy or sell, as written in your own plan.
- **Entry reason:** the exact condition you are waiting for.
- **Confirmation:** the final check that must happen before entry.
- **Planned risk:** the dollar amount calculated using your existing sizing method.
- **Stop:** the price recorded before entry.
- **Invalidation:** what would show that the original idea no longer matches your plan.

A **liquidity sweep** in this fictional example means price briefly moves below the earlier Asian low and then returns. A **breakout candle close** means the candle finishes beyond the level required by the trader's written setup. These descriptions explain the journal entry; they are not instructions to take a trade.

## What should you record after the trade closes?

After the trade, record facts first:

- actual entry and exit;
- result in dollars;
- result in **R**;
- whether the planned stop and exit method were followed;
- whether every setup condition was present;
- one [emotion-tracking](/glossary/emotion-tracking) tag;
- a chart screenshot; and
- one sentence about what you would repeat or change.

**R** is the amount you planned to risk on the trade. If planned risk was $50, a +$100 result is +2R. The R result makes trades with different dollar sizes easier to compare, but it still does not grade the quality of your decision.

Use simple labels. `Patient`, `rushed`, `frustrated`, or `confident` is easier to compare later than a long paragraph. The label is evidence about how you felt. It does not prove why the trade won or lost.

## A complete XAUUSD journal example

Here is one fictional entry:

**Before the trade**

- Instrument: XAUUSD
- Timeframe: M15
- Session: London
- Setup: Asian low liquidity sweep
- Confirmation: breakout candle closed as required by the written setup
- Planned entry: 2308.0
- Stop: 2305.5
- Planned risk: $50
- Invalidation: setup conditions no longer valid if the recorded stop is reached

**After the trade**

- Exit: 2313.0
- Result: +$100
- R result: +2R
- Plan followed: yes
- Feeling: patient
- Screenshot: saved with the journal entry
- Lesson: “The entry matched the plan; keep using the same written checks.”

The prices alone do not tell you whether this was a good decision. The useful evidence is that the trader recorded the setup before entry and compared the finished trade with that plan.

Now imagine the same trade made +$100 after an early entry that ignored the required candle close. The outcome is still profitable, but `plan followed` should be **no**. Do not let a win hide the rule break.

The opposite is also true. If every planned condition was present and the trade lost $50, the loss does not automatically make the decision wrong. Record the result, then review the process separately.

## How do you keep journaling from feeling like homework?

You may start with detailed notes and stop after a few days because every entry takes too long.

Use a three-minute record:

1. **What was the setup?** Name it.
2. **Did I follow it?** Write yes, partly, or no.
3. **How did I feel?** Choose one or two short tags.
4. **What is one lesson?** Write one sentence.

That is enough for a basic daily record. Add the prices, dollar result, R result, and screenshot as structured fields instead of rewriting them in a paragraph.

Start small. A short journal you complete is more useful than a complicated journal you avoid. If you want a ready-made record, use the [trading journal template](/blogs/trading-journal-template) with its downloadable CSV and worked entry.

## What should you review at the end of the week?

A journal becomes useful when you read it back. A [trade review](/glossary/trade-review) is a calm check of trades you already took. It is not a prediction of the next trade.

Ask five questions:

1. Which setup appeared most often?
2. Which mistake appeared more than once?
3. What was my strongest process habit?
4. Which session contained the weakest decisions?
5. What is one behaviour I will check next week?

## Common journal mistakes that hide the real problem

### Recording only profit and loss

A dollar result tells you what happened to the account. It does not tell you whether you waited for the setup, changed size, moved the stop, or traded outside your planned session.

### Writing only after losing trades

A winning trade can reinforce a bad habit. Record wins and losses with the same fields so the review does not reward every profitable rule break.

### Changing field names every week

If one entry says `rushed`, another says `too fast`, and another says `FOMO entry`, you may hide a repeated pattern. Keep a small list of setup and emotion labels and use them consistently.

### Writing an essay after every trade

Long notes can feel useful while you write them, but they are hard to compare. Keep the structured fields clear and use one sentence for the lesson.

### Looking for a signal in old results

Your journal can show what you did repeatedly. It cannot promise what XAUUSD will do next or guarantee that a setup will work in the future.

### Copying another trader's journal without checking your needs

Your journal should match your instruments, sessions, setups, review questions, and current prop-firm rules. Remove fields you never use. Add a field only when it answers a real review question.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best trading journal for prop firm traders?

The useful journal is the one you can complete and review consistently. It should record your plan, execution, result, rule adherence, emotion, screenshot, and lesson without creating so much work that you stop using it.

### Should I record every trade?

Use the same rule for all trades you want to compare. Recording only large wins or painful losses can hide ordinary decisions that form a repeated pattern.

### Is a screenshot enough for a trading journal?

No. A screenshot preserves the chart, but it may not show your planned risk, reason, emotion, or whether you followed your rules. Keep the screenshot beside a few structured fields.

### Should I write profit and loss in dollars or R?

Both can help. Dollars show the account result. R compares the result with the amount you planned to risk. Keep the calculation consistent and do not use either number as the only process score.

### How often should I review my journal?

Complete the short record after the trade while the details are clear. Then review several entries together at the end of your chosen week. A single entry shows one event; several entries can reveal repetition.

### Can a journal prevent rule breaches?

No. It can make your planned rules and past decisions easier to review, but it cannot control your next action or replace the firm's official risk controls.
